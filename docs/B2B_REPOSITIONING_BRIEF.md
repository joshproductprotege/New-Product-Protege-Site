## Executive Summary
Product Protégé today is a well-built B2C business. The book, the accelerator course, the certificate, the newsletter and the podcast are all designed to help an individual product manager get better at the craft — and they do that credibly. The site speaks, almost without exception, to the practitioner: “level up your product management game,” “enhance your career prospects,” “Analyst of Product to Associate PM to Senior PM and beyond.”
The objective is to flip the buyer. The economic decision to upskill a product organization is not made by the product manager; it is made by the CEO, the Chief Product Officer, the CTO and the VPs who own the P&L and the operating model. Those buyers do not wake up wanting “better PMs.” They wake up wanting faster time-to-market, higher return on their engineering spend, fewer expensive features nobody uses, and a team that can tell them which bets to make. The craft is the means; business outcomes are what they buy.
This brief delivers five things, in order: (1) a catalogue of every place the current site is mismatched to the new buyer, with specific fixes; (2) the marketing language and proof points that move C-suite and VP-level readers; (3) a repackaging of the existing assets into an enterprise offer ladder; (4) the rationale for each recommendation, rooted in what actually produces culture and operating-model change at scale; and (5) the positioning that resonates with product leaders alongside the economic case for everyone else in the C-suite.
Nothing here requires Product Protégé to throw away what it has built. The book, course, certificate and content are the raw material. They simply need to be re-aimed, re-packaged and re-priced for a buyer who thinks in quarters, headcount and outcomes rather than in personal skill progression.


## 1. Mismatch Catalogue: Where the Site Targets the Wrong Buyer
Each item below identifies the exact element on the current site, explains why it speaks to the practitioner rather than the decision-maker, and gives the B2B fix. Read the “Why it’s off” column as: “would a CPO or CEO see themselves in this?” In almost every case the answer today is no.

### 1.1 Hero and primary value proposition

### 1.2 The “Have you ever felt…” pain section
This section is the clearest example of B2C framing. Every pain is written in the first person of a junior or mid-level PM:
“Confused about what the product management role truly entails?”
“Every project feels wildly unpredictable and you’re not sure if you are adding any value…”

### 1.3 “Career Progression” as a headline benefit
The four-tile benefit row ends with “Career Progression — Advancing… from Analyst of Product to Associate PM to Senior PM and beyond.” This is a recruiting/retention message aimed at the employee. For a B2B buyer it is, at best, a footnote (“retention”), not a headline.
Fix: Replace “Career Progression” with an organizational capability outcome — e.g. “Organizational Capability: a repeatable way of working that survives reorgs, new hires and leadership changes.” Keep the individual-growth angle, but reframe it as a talent and retention benefit the leader cares about (lower attrition, faster ramp, stronger bench).

### 1.4 Testimonials and social proof

### 1.5 Product naming and information architecture
“Online Course & Certificate… get your Official Product Protégé Certificate” — a certificate is an individual credential. For B2B, lead with a cohort/enablement program; make the certificate a feature inside it, not the headline.
“Freebies” — the label is consumer-grade. Rename to “Resources” or “Diagnostics & Tools” and gate at least one high-value asset (a Product Operating Model assessment) behind a work email to generate qualified leads.
Cart / e-commerce checkout — a shopping cart signals a self-serve consumer purchase. Enterprise buyers expect “Book a working session” or “Request a proposal,” not “Add to cart.” Keep the cart for the book/course, but the primary CTA for org services must be a conversation, not a transaction.
“Product Advisor” — this is the most B2B-ready asset already on the site and should be elevated to the top of the navigation and reframed as the executive entry point (see Section 3).

### 1.6 Voice, channels and the “one PM to another” tone
The newsletter promises “real talk… from one PdM to another” and the podcast is about “making the magic of product management accessible to everyone.” This peer-to-peer, democratizing voice is exactly right for B2C and exactly wrong as the front door for a CPO. The fix is not to delete it — it is to create a second voice (an executive track) that speaks leader-to-leader about operating models, outcomes and ROI, while the peer voice continues to feed the practitioner top-of-funnel.

### 1.7 Domain framing: “eCommerce and beyond”
The book’s subtitle anchors the brand in eCommerce. Many enterprise buyers (fintech, healthcare, B2B SaaS, industrials) will self-select out. Reframe eCommerce as one proof domain among many, and lead the org offer with industry-neutral language: “any organization that builds software-enabled products and services.”

## 2. Marketing Language That Drives C-Suite and VP Leads
Senior leaders buy on outcomes, risk reduction and credibility — in that order. They are skeptical of “training” (it smells like a cost center) and they respond to “capability” and “operating model” (which smell like an investment with a return). Below is the language system to adopt.

### 2.1 Translate every practitioner phrase into a leadership phrase

### 2.2 The four messages that move each buyer
Tailor the lead message to the title. A single page can carry all four as a “For CEOs / For CPOs / For CTOs / For VPs” switcher.

#### For the CEO / Board
Predictable returns on technology investment — “know which bets are paying off, and kill the ones that aren’t, faster.”
Strategy that reaches the work — “your strategy survives contact with the roadmap instead of dissolving into a backlog.”
Resilience — “a way of working that doesn’t depend on a few heroes and survives reorgs.”

#### For the Chief Product Officer
From feature factory to outcome-driven — the central Marty Cagan / Melissa Perri narrative leaders already aspire to.
Empowered teams that you can actually trust with problems, not just tasks.
A discovery habit (Teresa Torres) that de-risks what gets built before money is spent building it.

#### For the CTO / VP Engineering
Less waste — “stop burning engineering capacity on features that don’t move metrics.”
Flow and lead time — the Jez Humble / DevOps lineage: small batches, fast feedback, measurable delivery.
Healthier product–engineering partnership — fewer thrash cycles, clearer problems, better specs.

#### For VPs of Product / Heads of Product
A scalable way to onboard and grow PMs without cloning yourself.
Consistency across squads — every team runs the same plays, so leadership reviews compare apples to apples.
Air cover — an external authority and method to drive change you’ve been trying to drive from the inside.

### 2.3 Proof points the C-suite actually weighs
Replace book reviews (as the primary org proof) with the four proof types enterprise buyers trust:
Outcome metrics. Before/after on cycle time, feature adoption, win rate, retention, time-to-first-value, % of roadmap tied to outcomes.
Named case studies. “How a 60-person product org moved from output to outcomes in two quarters,” with a leader’s quote and a measurable result.
Method credibility. A named, defensible methodology (E3 reframed as an operating-model journey) mapped to recognized thinking — Cagan’s empowered teams, Perri’s Build Trap, Torres’s discovery, Kotter’s change model.
Scale signals. Number of PMs trained, orgs transformed, cohorts run, and a coach bench — evidence Product Protégé can serve an enterprise, not just an individual.

## 3. Repackaging the Services for the New Buyer
Product Protégé already owns every ingredient an enterprise program needs: a method (E3), a book (the canonical text), a course + certificate (scalable enablement), coaching (behavior change), advisory (executive guidance) and content (demand generation). The work is to assemble them into an offer ladder that a leader can buy, sponsor and defend internally.

### 3.1 The enterprise offer ladder
Each rung is a natural step up in commitment and price. The lower rungs qualify leads and build trust; the upper rungs are where transformation — and revenue — actually happens.

### 3.2 How existing assets map to the ladder

### 3.3 Packaging principles
Sell capability, price the outcome. Name engagements after the result (“Outcome-Driven Roadmapping,” “Empowered Teams Program”), not the format (“6-week course”).
Make the diagnostic the universal entry point. Every leader, regardless of title, can say yes to an assessment. It is low-risk, fast, and produces a tailored case for change.
Bundle for the sponsor, not the seat. Price per cohort / per org with executive advisory included, so the CPO buys a transformation, not a stack of licenses.
Keep a self-serve lane. The book and individual course still serve practitioners and feed the brand; don’t cannibalize the funnel, just add the enterprise lane above it.


## 4. Rationale: What Actually Produces Change at Scale
Every recommendation above is anchored in established practice on how organizations adopt a product operating model and shift culture. This section makes that grounding explicit, so the recommendations can be defended to a skeptical executive.

### 4.1 You are selling an operating-model change, not a course
Marty Cagan (EMPOWERED, Transformed) and Melissa Perri (Escaping the Build Trap) both make the same point: organizations don’t become product-led by training individuals — they change how decisions are made, who is trusted with what, and what gets measured. That is why the offer must climb from training (Tier 3) to operating-model work (Tiers 1, 2, 5). A certificate alone changes a résumé; an operating model changes the company. Selling only the course leaves the transformation — and the value — on the table.

### 4.2 Change is led from the top and made real in the middle
Kotter’s change model is explicit that transformation needs a guiding coalition and visible executive sponsorship, while the new behaviors are anchored through repeated practice. This is the structural reason the buyer must be the C-suite (sponsorship, Tiers 1–2 and 5) even though the behavior change happens with teams (Tiers 3–4). A program sold to individual PMs has no sponsor and no air cover — it cannot change the system the PMs work inside.

### 4.3 Start small, prove value, then scale
Henrik Kniberg’s accounts of scaling (Spotify, Lego) and Kotter’s “short-term wins” both argue for incremental, evidence-generating change rather than big-bang rollouts. The offer ladder operationalizes this: the diagnostic and a single pilot cohort create the early wins that build the coalition for a wider rollout. It also de-risks the purchase for the buyer, which is how you close a skeptical executive.

### 4.4 Behavior change happens in the flow of work
Lyssa Adkins (coaching), Teresa Torres (continuous discovery habits) and Jeff Gothelf / Josh Seiden (Lean UX, outcome-based ways of working) converge on a single truth: durable change comes from changing what teams do every week, not from a one-time class. This is why embedded coaching (Tier 4) is essential and why “coaching sessions” should be repositioned from a transaction into ongoing, in-context behavior change. Training without coaching reverts; coaching makes it stick.

### 4.5 Measure outcomes, not output
John Doerr (OKRs), Jez Humble (flow and delivery metrics), and the Perri/Cagan outcome-over-output thesis all point to the same lever: what you measure is what changes. The diagnostic and every engagement should establish a small set of outcome metrics up front — so the buyer can see the return, and so the program proves itself. This is also the marketing proof the C-suite weighs (Section 2.3): you can only show outcome metrics if you instrument them from day one.

### 4.6 A common language is the cheapest scaling mechanism
Roman Pichler and Dan Olsen contribute the practical scaffolding — product vision, strategy, roadmap (Pichler) and product-market-fit / problem-space discipline (Olsen). At scale, the value of a shared framework like E3 is not the framework itself but the common language it gives dozens of teams, so leadership reviews become comparable and coordination cost drops. John Cutler’s work on operating models reinforces that consistency-with-autonomy is what lets a product org scale without fragmenting. This is why the certificate and cohort model matter for B2B: they manufacture shared language across the org.


## 5. Positioning: The Product Case and the Economic Case
To win the room, Product Protégé needs two complementary narratives running at once: one that makes product leaders feel understood and ambitious, and one that gives non-product executives a hard-nosed economic reason to fund the work.

### 5.1 The positioning statement

### 5.2 Resonating with product leaders (the aspiration)
Product leaders already know the destination — they read Cagan and Perri. They need to believe Product Protégé can get their org there. Lead with the transformation they aspire to:
From feature factory to outcome-driven product org.
From roadmaps of demands to a portfolio of bets tied to business results.
From PMs as backlog administrators to empowered teams trusted with problems.
From discovery as an afterthought to a continuous habit that de-risks spend.
From change that depends on heroes to a model that survives reorgs and new hires.

### 5.3 The economic case (for everyone outside product)
This is what unlocks budget. Translate the product mindset into the financial language a CEO, CFO or board uses. Frame each as “you are already paying for the absence of this.”

### 5.4 Holding both audiences without losing either
Product Protégé does not have to abandon its practitioner community to win leaders — and shouldn’t, because that community is credibility and demand generation. The clean solution is a two-door site:
Leadership door (new primary): operating model, outcomes, ROI, diagnostic, advisory. Aimed at CEO / CPO / CTO / VP.
Practitioner door (existing): book, course, certificate, newsletter, podcast. Aimed at PMs — and quietly feeding leaders the proof that their teams already respect this method.
The practitioner content becomes a moat: when a CPO evaluates Product Protégé, their own PMs already know and trust the brand. That bottom-up familiarity is a genuine advantage few transformation consultancies have — lean into it rather than hiding it.


## 6. Suggested Sequence of Action
A pragmatic order of operations, smallest effort and highest leverage first.
Rewrite the hero and the “Have you ever felt…” section to leadership pains and outcomes. Highest-leverage change; touches the first screen every buyer sees.
Stand up a Product Operating Model Diagnostic as a gated asset and the universal CTA. Turns the site into a lead engine for leaders.
Elevate “Product Advisor” to the top of navigation and reframe as the executive entry point.
Reframe the offer as the five-tier ladder and replace “Add to cart” with “Request a proposal” for org services.
Add outcome-based case studies and leader testimonials with metrics; demote book reviews to the practitioner door.
Launch an executive content track (briefings, the economic case) alongside the existing peer newsletter/podcast.
Build the two-door information architecture (Leadership / Practitioner) once the messaging is proven.
